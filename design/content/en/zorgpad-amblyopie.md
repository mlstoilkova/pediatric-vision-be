Amblyopia care pathway

What is amblyopia?
Amblyopia, also called “lazy eye”, is reduced visual acuity in one or, rarely, both eyes that cannot be fully corrected with glasses.
Amblyopia develops in early childhood, during the critical period of visual development. This period starts at birth and lasts until about age 10, while the visual cortex develops. An abnormal visual experience can disrupt this development for one eye, or sometimes both, leading to unilateral or bilateral amblyopia. Amblyopia is reversible if recognised and treated in time; left untreated, it leads to permanent visual impairment.

Main causes
> 1. Strabismic amblyopia – caused by a squint, where the image from one eye is suppressed.
> 2. Refractive (anisometropic) amblyopia – caused by an unequal prescription between the two eyes.
> 3. Deprivation amblyopia – caused by an obstruction of vision (e.g. congenital cataract, ptosis).

1. Detection and screening
- Age: early detection is essential, ideally before age 6. Unilateral amblyopia is often missed by those around the child, because seeing with both eyes causes no spontaneous complaints.
- Who detects it?
  > Kind en Gezin / ONE during routine visits
  > CLB
  > GP or paediatrician in case of visual complaints
  > Parents who notice a squint or unusual looking behaviour
- Warning signs: squint, closing one eye, abnormal head posture, blurred vision, complaints when one eye is covered.
- Unilateral amblyopia causes no spontaneous complaints and is often missed by those around the child!

2. Referral to a specialist
- When?
  > Abnormal screening test, family history of amblyopia or squint
  > Abnormal red reflex
  > Always when a squint or a difference in visual acuity between the eyes is suspected
  > With risk factors: family history of squint, congenital cataract, high prescription

3. Diagnosis in the eye clinic
- Full eye examination: age-appropriate visual acuity, fundoscopy, motility, stereo tests.
- Cycloplegic refraction: detecting refractive errors (hypermetropia, anisometropia, astigmatism).
- Additional tests: only in case of an atypical course or suspected organic cause.

4. Treatment
- Correcting the refractive error
  > First step: prescribing the right glasses.
  > Vision often improves considerably within 4–12 weeks.
- Occlusion therapy (patching)
  > Patching the good eye to stimulate the lazy eye.
  > Duration and intensity depend on age and severity.
  > Alternative in case of compliance problems: atropine drops in the good eye
- Treating the underlying cause
  > Strabismus surgery if the squint persists after correction with glasses.
  > Surgery for cataract/ptosis.

5. Follow-up
- Frequency:
  > Every 2–3 months for deep amblyopia, every 6 months for mild amblyopia or significant risk factors, yearly for stable children
  > Until stabilisation and maximum visual acuity are reached
- Checkpoints:
  > Visual acuity of both eyes
  > Treatment compliance (patching time, acceptance)
  > Orthoptic parameters (angle of squint, fusion, stereo vision)

6. End of treatment
- Goal: maximum visual gain before age 8–10
- Yearly check-ups for patients at risk (high prescriptions, family history of strabismus).

7. Role of the GP and primary care
- Stay alert to visual complaints, unusual looking behaviour and family problems
- Refer in time when an abnormality is suspected.
- Support parents with treatment adherence (patching, wearing glasses).
- Recognise the psychosocial impact (bullying, refusing treatment, socially vulnerable families).
